## Why Spain has a higher win probability than Argentina despite being ranked lower

The apparent contradiction resolves once you recognise that **the ranking and the win probability are computed from different quantities at different time points**.

### 1. The ranking is *post-tournament*; the prediction is *pre-final*

- The **post-tournament ranking** is computed **after** all matches (including the final) are assimilated.
- The **final-match prediction** is a one-step-ahead forecast made **before** the final's result enters the filter. The code uses `filtered_states.particles.x[t+1]` at the prediction day — i.e. the state after the semi-finals, not after the final.

So the two are not even at the same time point.

### 2. Total strength ≠ win probability

The ranking is by **total strength** (attack + defence). But the win probability is determined by the **cross-matchup** in the bivariate-Poisson scoring rates:

$$\log\lambda_1 = \alpha + (x^{\text{att}}_{\text{home}} - x^{\text{def}}_{\text{away}}), \qquad \log\lambda_2 = \alpha + (x^{\text{att}}_{\text{away}} - x^{\text{def}}_{\text{home}}).$$

A team's win probability depends on **its attack against the opponent's defence** and **its defence against the opponent's attack** — not on the sum of its own two components. A team ranked lower in total strength can still have a favourable matchup.

### 3. There is no home-advantage term in the model

The model is symmetric: the "home" designation only picks which team is $x_i$. So the higher Spain win probability is purely a matchup effect, not a home-field effect.

### 4. The concrete numbers (post-tournament strengths, for illustration)

| | Spain | Argentina |
|---|---|---|
| Attack | 0.9095 | 1.5040 |
| Defence | 0.7531 | 1.1860 |
| Total (rank) | 1.6626 (22) | 2.6900 (9) |

Cross-matchup scoring rates:
- Spain's rate: $\alpha + (0.9095 - 1.1860) = \alpha - 0.2765$
- Argentina's rate: $\alpha + (1.5040 - 0.7531) = \alpha + 0.7509$

By these post-tournament values, Argentina's scoring rate is higher — which would predict an Argentina win. **But the actual prediction uses the pre-final state**, where the matchup is different. The fact that the model predicts Spain to win reflects the pre-final strengths, not the post-tournament ones.

### The deeper point for the dissertation

This is actually **further evidence that the SQMC total-strength ranking is not a faithful summary of the model's predictive beliefs**. The ranking collapses the full posterior to a single post-tournament ordering, while the win probability reflects the pre-final attack–defence matchup. The two need not agree, and their disagreement is part of why the SQMC ranking is hard to interpret qualitatively.
